## Game Notes — as if nobody has ever played this game

Chess is a slow, thoughtful battle between two armies on a board with 64 squares, eight rows across and eight rows down, coloured light and dark like a checkerboard floor. One army is White and the other army is Black. Each army has 16 pieces: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and eight pawns. White always moves first. The aim is not to capture every piece. The aim is to trap the enemy king so that it cannot escape. That trapped position is called checkmate, and the player who checkmates the other king wins the game. If neither king can be trapped, and the board reaches a position where no legal move can change the result, the game can end in a draw, which means nobody wins and nobody loses. This phone version is called the Grand Heritage edition, so the board looks like marble and brass, with carved pieces that feel old, grand, and calm, and the game speaks in quiet messages like "Aisha plays Nf3" to tell you what just happened.

When the game opens, you see a splash screen that says WAJIHA, then CHESS, then THE GRAND HERITAGE EDITION, then Ready! After that comes the main menu. The menu shows the word CHESS, the line "The royal game, in marble & brass", and buttons for Play vs Computer, Two Players, Settings, Chess PRO, Share, Rate, and Support the club. You will also see a choice for whether you play White or Black in a computer game. That choice matters, because White moves first, so playing White feels a little easier at the start, while playing Black means you answer White's first move. If a game was left half-finished, the menu may offer Resume game, with Resume and Discard choices. Resume carries on the old game. Discard throws it away and lets you start fresh, so only tap Discard if you really do not want that old game back.

To start, tap Play vs Computer if you want to play against the phone, or Two Players if two people want to share the phone and take turns. In a computer game, first pick your colour, White or Black, and your Bot difficulty in Settings or on the menu setup: Casual is the friendliest computer, Club Player is a stronger club-level computer, and Grandmaster is the sharpest computer and a Pro feature, locked without Pro. In a two-player game, both people play on the same phone, passing it back and forth, and there is no computer opponent at all. Then tap to start, and the board appears with all 32 pieces in their starting places. White pieces sit on the first two rows at one end, Black pieces sit on the first two rows at the other end, and the pawns form a wall in front of their own army.

Each piece moves in its own special way, and learning those six moves is learning chess itself, so here they are, one by one, in plain words. A pawn moves one square straight forward, never backwards, and never sideways, except on its very first move, when it may move two squares straight forward if both squares are empty. A pawn captures diagonally, one square forward and one square to the side, never straight ahead. A rook moves any number of empty squares straight, forwards, backwards, or sideways, like a car driving along a road. A bishop moves any number of empty squares diagonally, like a car driving diagonally across the board, and it always stays on the same colour square it started on. A queen moves like a rook and a bishop combined: any number of empty squares straight or diagonally, in any direction, which is why the queen is the most powerful piece. A knight jumps in an L shape: two squares in one straight direction, then one square to the side, and it is the only piece that can jump over other pieces standing in its way. A king moves one square in any direction: straight, sideways, or diagonally, but only one square at a time. No piece may jump over another piece except the knight, and no piece may land on a square already holding one of your own pieces.

Playing goes like this, turn by turn. First, look at the board and find one of your pieces. Second, tap that piece. The game will light up the squares where that piece is allowed to go, so you do not have to guess. Third, tap one of the lit squares. Your piece moves there, with a soft sound. If that square held an enemy piece, your piece captures it, and the enemy piece leaves the board. If the square was empty, your piece simply moves there. Fourth, the turn passes to the other player, and the same steps repeat. That is all there is to a single move, but the thinking behind each move is the game. You are always trying to protect your king, attack the enemy king, and win valuable pieces without losing your own for nothing.

Three special rules need their own careful explanations, because new players always ask about them. Castling is a special king move. If your king and one of your rooks have never moved, and the squares between them are empty, and your king is not in check and does not pass through check, you may castle by moving the king two squares towards that rook, and the rook jumps over to the square beside the king, all in one move. In this game you castle simply by moving the king two squares, and the game moves the rook for you. Promotion happens when a pawn reaches the very last row on the enemy side. A pawn cannot stay a pawn there. It must become a new queen, rook, bishop, or knight, and you choose which piece from a small choice panel that says Choose your piece. Most players choose a queen, because it is the strongest, but sometimes a knight is cleverer because of its jumping move. Check happens when your king is under attack, meaning an enemy piece could capture it on the next move. The game shows a CHECK banner, and you must get out of check on your next move, by moving the king away, capturing the attacking piece, or blocking the attack with another piece. You may not make a move that leaves your own king in check. Checkmate happens when your king is in check and there is no legal move at all that gets it out of check. That ends the game at once, and the player who delivered checkmate wins.

Winning, losing, and drawing work like this. You win by checkmating the enemy king. You lose if your king is checkmated, or if you tap Resign and give up, which is an honest way to end a hopeless game without playing it to the bitter end. You draw if the game reaches stalemate, which means the player whose turn it is has no legal move, but their king is not in check. You can also draw by agreement: in a two-player game, one player can offer a draw with the Draw button, and the other player taps Accept or Decline. If they accept, the game ends as a draw. If they decline, the game carries on. The game also tracks moves in a Moves panel, so you can look back at the moves played, for example White played e4, Black answered, White played Nf3, and so on, written in short chess writing. The Undo button takes back the last move, which is useful when you are learning, or when you tap the wrong square by accident in a friendly game, but check in the game whether Undo is allowed in every mode, because a serious computer game may limit it, and you should not promise a tester that Undo always works in all modes.

Here is a worked example with simple moves, using two made-up players, Layla playing White and Imran playing Black. Layla taps her pawn in front of her king and moves it two squares forward, to the centre. Imran taps his pawn in front of his king and moves it two squares forward as well. Layla taps her knight and jumps it in an L shape towards the centre, where it attacks a square near Imran's side. Imran taps his knight and jumps it out too. Later Layla moves her queen to a square where it attacks Imran's king, and the game shows CHECK, so Imran must answer the check at once, perhaps by moving his king one square away, or by blocking with a piece. If Imran later has no move that saves his king, the game shows checkmate, and Layla wins, even though many pieces may still be left on the board. In a different example, Layla's pawn reaches the last row, the game asks Choose your piece, Layla taps the queen picture, and her pawn turns into a second queen, which is a joyful moment and often the move that wins the game.

The chess clock is an extra choice in Settings, and it is not turned on by default. The clock choices are No clock, 1 min, 3 min, 5 min, and 10 min. With No clock, you can think as long as you like on every move, which is best for learning. With a clock, each player has that much total time for the whole game, and the clock ticks down only on your turn. If your time runs out, check in the game exactly what the end screen says and whether the game calls it a loss on time, because the engine has a flag event for a clock flag, but the exact words on the end screen should be confirmed in a real timed game, not assumed. The game screen also shows a BOT label during a computer turn, so you know when the phone is thinking, and a Resume and Pause control for stopping and starting again.

If you make a wrong move, several things can happen, and they are different, so do not mix them up. If you tap a piece and then change your mind before tapping a destination square, you can usually tap a different piece instead, and nothing has moved yet, so that is not a wrong move at all. If you tap a destination square that is not lit, nothing happens, and the board does not change. If you tap a lit square and your piece moves there, that move is played, and your only way back is the Undo button, if it is available in that mode. If there is no Undo in that mode, the move stands, and you must play on from the new position. That is why chess players always look at the lit squares and think for a second before they tap. A wrong move in chess never costs you a life or a point directly. It only makes your position worse, which may lead to losing a piece, falling into check, or eventually being checkmated.

Sound and appearance live in Settings. Settings has Bot difficulty, Play White or Play Black, Chess clock, player names for White and Black, Board theme, Piece style, Music, described as menu and game ambience, and Sound effects, described as moves, captures, and alerts. You can also reach How to play from Settings, which repeats the short instructions: tap a piece, then tap a highlighted square, castle by moving the king two squares, promote pawns on the last rank, checkmate to win, stalemate is a draw. The Pro screen lists what Pro adds: all board themes, extra piece styles, and the Grandmaster bot. Board themes include Grand Heritage, Royal Mahogany, Ebony and Ivory, Midnight Library, Emerald Club, and others, and piece styles include Classic Carved, Modern Minimal, and Ornate Royal. Your settings, your names, your theme, your piece style, your clock choice, and your lifetime tally against the computer are saved on your device, so they survive closing and reopening the game. To exit, pause the game, choose Quit to menu, or finish the game and tap Back to menu, then leave the app with your phone's normal home control.

## What to Test — for TestFlight

Start on the menu and confirm the title, the Play vs Computer and Two Players buttons, the White or Black choice, the Settings and Chess PRO buttons, and the Resume game offer, if one appears, all work without a blank screen. Start a Play vs Computer game as White on Casual and confirm White moves first, the BOT label appears on the computer's turn, and the move narration names the move played. Play a full win against the computer if you can, or set up a two-player game and play a short checkmate with a friend, and confirm the end screen declares the winner and offers Rematch and Back to menu, with Rematch starting a fresh board. Play a full loss on purpose by letting the computer checkmate you, or by tapping Resign in a losing position, and confirm the loss screen and the Resign confirmation, with Keep playing as a safe way back if you change your mind.

Test each piece's move: move a pawn one square, then in a fresh game move a pawn two squares on its first move, move a rook along a row, move a bishop diagonally, move a queen straight and diagonally, jump a knight over a piece in an L shape, and move a king one square, and confirm lit squares only allow legal moves and an unlit square tap does nothing. Test castling: in a two-player game, clear the squares between king and rook, move the king two squares, and confirm the rook jumps beside the king. Test promotion: advance a pawn to the last rank and confirm the Choose your piece panel appears and the pawn becomes the piece you tapped. Test check: put a king in check and confirm the CHECK banner appears and illegal moves that leave the king in check are refused. Test stalemate or a draw offer: in a two-player game, tap Draw, confirm the other player sees Accept and Decline, accept once and confirm a draw, and in another game decline and confirm play continues.

Test the wrong-move cases: tap a piece, then tap a different piece before moving, and confirm no move is played. Play a bad move on purpose, tap Undo, and note whether Undo works in that mode, because that point is marked check in the game. Test Restart from the pause panel with Start over, confirming Cancel keeps the game and Restart clears it. Test the clock: set a 1-minute clock, play a game, and note exactly what happens when time runs out, because that wording is marked check in the game. Test sound: turn Music off and confirm the ambience stops but move sounds remain, turn Sound effects off and confirm move sounds stop, and change Board theme and Piece style and confirm the board actually changes. Test difficulty: play Casual, then Club Player, and confirm Club Player plays more sharply, and confirm Grandmaster is locked without Pro. Test exit and reopen: quit a game to the menu, close the app completely, reopen it, and confirm your settings, names, theme, and tally are still there, and note whether a half-finished game offered Resume.
